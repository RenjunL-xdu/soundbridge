How it works

Data is carried on many simultaneous audio tones — the same idea a dial-up modem or Wi-Fi uses, played through a speaker and picked up by a microphone.

It measures the room first

Before sending anything, the two devices exchange a frequency sweep. The receiver measures signal quality at every frequency and reports back. Frequencies that are noisy, blocked, or simply not reproduced by your speaker are dropped; the clear ones carry more bits each. This is why it adapts to whatever hardware and room you have.

Losing sound is fine

Packets are encoded with a fountain code. The sender emits an endless stream of distinct combinations, and the receiver needs any sufficient number of them — not any particular ones. If someone talks, a door slams, or the phone is nudged, the transfer gets slightly longer rather than failing. No retransmission requests are needed.

The narrower the band, the less room there is for data. That is the whole trade: silence costs speed. The app picks the quietest band that still meets your time budget.

On your ears

Transmit power is shaped by the ear's own sensitivity curve, which peaks sharply around 3–4 kHz. Energy is pushed toward frequencies the ear treats as dull, so the same data rate is markedly less irritating. Levels are kept well below anything hazardous, bursts are ramped rather than switched on, and peaks are controlled so the speaker never clips. Even so: keep the volume moderate, and note that children and pets hear considerably more of this than you do.

Honest limits

Usable audio bandwidth is about 20 kHz, and only a sliver of it is inaudible. That caps throughput at a few kilobytes per second. Small files — keys, configs, text, documents — move in seconds. A 10 MB file is realistically tens of minutes, and the app tells you the estimate before starting rather than after.

Bluetooth speakers and headsets will not work. Their codecs discard exactly this part of the spectrum. Use built-in speakers and microphones.
